The Actuator Model Problem: Why Sim-to-Real Fails at the Motor

Every RL policy trains against an actuator model, and the industry default is wrong. We tested it against real motor physics on a quadruped and a humanoid. The humanoid trained on the default falls in 4,554 of 4,554 episodes; trained on our model, it walks with zero falls. The same model works in reverse: hardware choices graded by the policies they produce, before the hardware exists.

Pranav Myana · Atlas Motion Systems

July 2026

Every RL policy trains against some model of its actuators. The default is a PD controller with a torque clamp: thirty newton-meters, instantly, at any speed, forever. A real motor ramps current through inductance, loses torque with speed, and derates with heat. The policy spends 150 million steps rehearsing habits the hardware can't honor.

Atlas builds actuator design tools, and a finished design already contains every number a faithful simulation needs. So we built atlas_actuators, an open-source Isaac Lab plugin that turns an Atlas design into a physics-accurate training actuator, and measured what the default costs:

  1. On a humanoid, the mismatch is fatal. The stock G1 policy falls in essentially every episode on real physics. Ours walks with zero falls in 256 episodes; a second training seed lands at 99.7 versus 4.5 percent.
  2. Hardware iteration becomes a compile-test loop. We swept gearboxes like learning rates for about a dollar each. Because Atlas manufactures the actuator, the design you converge on is the one that ships.
  3. Fidelity costs nothing at train time: 26.0 versus 26.2 in its own world, averaged over three seeds.
  4. The bill arrives at transfer: 4x the falls, pooled over three seeds, on flat ground.
  5. Better training does not close a model gap. A hardened curriculum cut falls about 7x for both policies; the mismatched one still falls 2.2x as often.
  6. The training model, the safety layer, and the shipped firmware are one law, matched to 1.1e-6.

What an actuator actually is

An actuator is a motor, a gearbox, and a drive: power electronics and a microcontroller that sit between every command and every motion. The motor is magnets on a rotor chasing currents in stator windings. Making torque means steering those currents thousands of times a second as the rotor turns, which is the drive's job: field-oriented control at 20-40 kHz, turning "give me thirty newton-meters" into three timed phase currents while watching current, rotor angle, and winding temperature. The gearbox trades motor speed for joint torque.

The robot's computer talks to each drive a few hundred times per second; the drive handles everything faster on its own. So a simulator that models "the actuator" is modeling this whole closed-loop stack: motor physics, current controller, limits. The default model throws all of it away in favor of a torque genie.

Every simulator ships a fantasy motor

The one strong model in the toolbox is ETH's 2019 actuator net [1]: hours of measured torque on their ANYdrive, distilled into an LSTM. It transferred famously, Isaac Lab ships it today [2], and it describes exactly one actuator. The method needs the hardware to exist first, so it can't answer the question that matters at design time: whether this motor is worth building at all.

The hardware record is blunt. Policies trained with an ideal or analytical actuator model "could not make a single step without falling" on ANYmal, and that was after ETH spent a week tuning the analytical model [1]. On TOCABI, a full-size humanoid with 100:1 harmonic drives, every training seed of the naive baseline failed to control the robot [3]. On a NAO whose simulator answered commands instantly, seven of ten policies improved in sim and degraded the real walk [4]. Google's Minitaur "fell to the ground immediately" until torque saturation and latency entered the simulation; domain randomization alone did not save it [5]. Transfer survives in proportion to how honestly the training world models the actuator.

The model is a design output

A finished Atlas design knows its torque constant, resistance, inductance, current limits, and thermal resistances. That is everything needed to simulate what the drive's microcontroller will actually run, current loop included. What simpler models miss is the price of torque at speed:

Two checks keep it honest: energy conservation closes to 0.00 percent, and the control law compiles to microcontroller C that replays against the simulation to within 1.1e-6. The simulator and the firmware are the same law.

The plugin enters Isaac Lab through the same ActuatorBase socket as ETH's network. One is distilled from measurements of a machine that exists. The other is derived from the design of one that does not.

The A/B test

The quadruped is the controlled study: statically stable, so whatever the actuator swap changes is attributable to the model. ANYmal-C, identical everything, 1,500 iterations. Policy A trains on the stock actuator net, policy B on our FOC model. Both are evaluated on the FOC physics, which stands in for the robot you actually built.

That was one seed, so we ran two more with fresh training and ~260-episode evals for every cell. Transfer falls 5.0 / 6.3 / 5.8 percent across seeds 42/43/44; the matched policy falls 2.7 / 0.9 / 1.5. Every seed shows the penalty, and the original 2.6x turns out to be the most flattering draw of the three. Own-world parity replicates: 26.0 against 26.2 on average.

Co-design for a dollar a gearbox

Hardware design has always been open-loop with respect to behavior: size the motor, build the robot, learn the truth months later. Software has iterated inside a compile-test loop for fifty years. Actuators never had one, because grading hardware by the behavior it produces required the hardware to exist. A design-time actuator model breaks that dependency:

hardware iterationtraditionalwith a design-time model
one lap of the loopmonths~30 minutes
cost per lapa hardware revision~$1 of GPU time
behavior knownafter the buildbefore the first part is machined
graded bydatasheet marginsfalls, tracking, thermals of a trained policy
who iteratesmechanical engineeringanyone with a GPU

The loop ends in something real because Atlas is not a design tool with a simulator attached. We manufacture the actuator. A roboticist iterating in this loop is co-designing the exact unit that will arrive, its training twin already the spec sheet, its firmware already the law the policy trained against. Simulator vendors do not build motors. Motor catalogs do not ship training models. The loop only closes when the same party does both, and today that list has one name on it.

We ran the loop on the gearbox: three ratios, one policy each, graded in their own worlds.

One line makes the same axis discriminate: raise the command range from 1 m/s to 2.5 and the tie breaks. The 6:1 posts the highest reward and the most falls. The 12:1 gives back two reward points and buys a third fewer falls. The 9:1, the safe middle choice on a datasheet, loses to the 12:1 on both axes. Flat-ground cruise could not rank three gearboxes; three dollars of sprint eliminated the middle of the catalog.

The loop runs the other way too. Hand-rescaling the humanoid's PD gains to the torque ratio made everything worse: the optimizer, training through the real model, had already found a better operating point.

The last axis is heat, which no stock simulator models at all. Every joint in our model carries a winding-temperature state (I²R heating, Newton cooling) and a derate that fades the current ceiling as the winding heats. We built a task where the constraint binds, two minutes of commanded sprinting against a forty-second thermal time constant, and trained the same warm-started walker twice: once blind, once with its twelve winding temperatures in the observation. The obvious prediction is that the aware policy learns to slow down. It does not. It walks the same 1.7 m/s with its hottest winding eleven degrees cooler, spreading load so no single winding becomes the fuse, and falls 4 times to the blind policy's 17. Thermal load-balancing appears in no reward term. It is what the optimizer does once the policy can feel heat and the physics makes heat cost something. Heat is not hypothetical: agile-skill data collection on deployed G1s has ended with joint motors overheated to hardware failure [6].

The gearbox was the visible knob. The task reaches much deeper than that, because every layer of the metal is the answer to a question the software asks. Peak torque at speed, the shove-recovery currency, is a voltage problem: the winding gets fewer turns of thicker wire, trading torque per amp for headroom against back-EMF. A steady payload carry asks the opposite question, so the same stator gets wound with more turns, buying torque per amp and cutting resistive heat at continuous duty. Magnet thickness follows the continuous magnetic loading and the demagnetization margin at peak current. Back iron exists to carry the flux the magnets push, so it thickens in lockstep with them, and a high pole count keeps flux per pole small enough that both rims stay thin. Gear teeth are sized by the peak tooth loads in the winning policy's own torque history; how much backlash the gears are allowed is priced by what play costs the policy (half a degree, 14.3 percent of episodes). Even the sensor list is task-derived: encoder resolution is priced by what velocity quantization costs the policy, and the winding thermistors earn their BOM line because a policy that can feel them falls four times instead of seventeen. To make this concrete, we gave the design generator two tasks for the same joint envelope and let it answer:

Same envelope, same voltage rail, same slot-pole skeleton; different copper, different magnets, different iron. Nothing in that table was hand-chosen. The tasks were. The robot's software is now a load case, the same way gravity is, and an actuator vendor that cannot run this loop is guessing at half the loads.

The humanoid does not get up

A quadruped stumbles onto its other legs; a humanoid balances or it does not exist. And its baseline is worse than idealized: the stock G1 task drives every joint at 300 N·m when the real knee peaks at 139. Marketed versus achieved, for torque, baked into every G1 policy that task has produced. Our variant gears one Atlas motor three ways (12:1 legs, matching the real knee; 2:1 ankles and arms) and changes nothing else.

The gait chase became evidence in its own right. The stock reward is satisfied by a crouch, on any torque:

Getting the right-hand clip produced the sharpest finding here. The posture reward fixed the fantasy world on the first try. Trained from scratch on real physics, it produced a policy that stands, perfectly stable, and never walks. Fantasy torque does not just distort the gait you get; it changes which behaviors are reachable.

The last two rows close open loops. Training against shoves does for the humanoid what it did for the quadruped: 0.4 percent falls at ±1.0, one fall in 512 episodes. The actuator model also now covers two effects the actuator net used to hold over physics: a 14-bit motor-shaft encoder (velocity from finite differences of the quantized signal) and 8 mrad of gear play, modeled as a play operator whose gap traversal costs the physical fraction of a control step. Half a degree of backlash is not cosmetic; after 1,500 adaptation iterations it still costs 14.3 percent of unpushed episodes. Our first backlash model gated torque for a full control step per reversal, about 10x the physical dead time, and collapsed the policy. Video review caught it, and the corrected model is the one reported here.

The scaling is uncomfortable. The quadruped pays mismatch as a 2x robustness tax; the humanoid pays it as nonexistence. The industry is migrating from the forgiving platform to the unforgiving one.

Actuator net or physics? Wrong question.

On an ANYdrive, ETH's network is almost certainly more accurate; it absorbed friction and backlash that first principles miss. The right metric is transfer quality per dollar and week of characterization:

actuator net (ETH)physics-from-design (Atlas)
needs the hardware to existyes, plus a torque-instrumented rigno
marginal cost per new actuatorweeks of collection and fittingzero; it is a design output
captures friction, backlash, comm delaysyes, when the data covers thembacklash and encoder quantization modeled from design parameters; friction and delays arrive through fleet telemetry
extrapolates to a new bus voltage, a hotter winding, a gear swapsilent; it never saw that dataanswers from physics
thermal state, temperature-aware policiesnonewinding temperature per joint, observable by the policy
auditableblack boxevery parameter is a design quantity; energy-balance checked
doubles as the deployed controllerno; a simulation-only artifactthe same law compiles to the drive firmware, at 1e-6 parity

The approaches compose. The endgame is a learned residual on a physics prior, and it belongs to whoever builds the actuator. ETH needed a rig to characterize someone else's black box from outside. We are inside, where comparing commanded against delivered torque at 20 kHz is what FOC already does. ETH characterized one actuator, once. A fleet that ships with its own characterization loop does it continuously, and every residual flows back into the training twin.

The field's other routes agree on the diagnosis and pick different medicine. Berkeley Humanoid constrains the hardware to low gear ratios so the ideal model becomes nearly true [7]. Booster measures each actuator's torque-speed envelope on a bench and pastes the knee points into simulation [8]. Recent work reshapes the real actuator's own controller until it imitates the simulator's reference model [9]. Each treats the actuator model as the product. Ours is the same conclusion with a different origin: the model is emitted by the tool that designed the motor.

The permanent layer

As robots get more neural, the engineered actuator layer does not fade. It becomes the most important layer on the robot.

The physics floor does not move. Policies run at 50-100 Hz; commutation runs at 20-40 kHz with hard real-time guarantees. No network does that job, and no gradient makes it worth trying. The more the stack neuralizes, the more this is the only engineered interface left.

Every policy trains against an actuator model; the only question is whose. In a hand-tuned world a datasheet model was fine because an engineer compensated. In an RL world the model is the product interface. RL does not route around your characterization data. It consumes it.

A learned policy cannot be the safety layer. Thermal limits, overcurrent, joint limits, sanity when the policy outputs garbage: these need guarantees, and networks do not issue them. That layer lives in firmware, which in our case is the same law the simulation runs. The thing that protects the hardware, the thing the policy trains against, and the thing that ships are one object.

Today those three floors belong to three vendors plus glue, and the sim-to-real gap is the seam where the glue fails. Atlas's position is one surface: we design and manufacture the motor, the same computation produces the training twin, the firmware is the identical law, and every deployed unit feeds telemetry back into the model every future policy trains against.

What's next

Three seeds on the quadruped, two on the humanoid, one each on the hardware-effects and thermal results; the splits are large enough that more seeds would sharpen error bars, not conclusions. The model grows where the data arrives: gear friction and bus latency land with fleet telemetry, and the backlash operator graduates from first-order play when a real gearbox gets measured. The next artifact is a drive board, a motor, and a load cell, about $300 in parts, the emitted firmware flashed unmodified, and the measured torque-speed curve laid over Fig 2. That plot settles the argument in whichever direction it chooses.
